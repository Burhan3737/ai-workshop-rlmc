A practical playbook for durable medical-exam learning—built around what cognitive science consistently supports.

01
Active recall

Make your brain
produce the answer.

Retrieval practice means answering without looking. The effort is not a sign of failure—it is part of the mechanism.

  • Close the notes. Write a mechanism, differential, or management step from memory.
  • Then check. Correct gaps immediately and turn them into your next questions.
  • Use exam-shaped prompts. Flashcards, vignettes, and blank-page recall all count.
A classic finding
Test > restudy

In a delayed test, repeatedly testing learned vocabulary produced a large benefit; extra study after correct recall did not.

What is the first-line investigation?
Cover answer → retrieve → check → repair

Karpicke & Roediger, Science (2008) [1]

02
Spaced repetition

Return just before
you forget.

Spread retrieval opportunities across time. Distributed practice reliably improves later recall over cramming, and the useful gap depends on how long you need to retain it.

TodayLearn + retrieve
Day 1Quick recall
Day 3Mixed questions
Day 7Vignettes
Day 14+Exam review

Use this as a starting cadence, not a law. Keep hard cards closer; let fluent, correct material drift farther apart. A large meta-analysis found that the best interval changes with the final retention interval.

Cepeda et al., Psychological Bulletin (2006) [2]

03
Interleaving

Mix the cases.
Choose the strategy.

Blocking repeats one pattern until it feels fluent. Interleaving makes you identify which approach a new problem actually needs.

  • Mix similar-looking conditions: e.g., microcytic anemias, acid–base disorders, or murmurs.
  • After a short learning block, shuffle 2–3 related topics into one question set.
  • Expect it to feel harder during practice; that is not the same as learning less.

Mixed mathematics practice led to substantially better delayed-test performance than blocked practice in a controlled study.

Rohrer & Taylor, Instructional Science (2007) [3]

04
A 45-minute study block

Turn one topic into
a durable memory.

0–12 min

Understand

Read a small unit. Sketch the causal chain or teach it simply. Do not keep rereading.

12–30 min

Retrieve

Close everything. Answer prompts, make a quick brain dump, or solve clinical questions.

30–45 min

Mix + schedule

Add a few related old questions. Mark misses for a near review; defer secure items.

At the end, ask one calibration question: “Could I answer this tomorrow with no cues?” If not, it belongs on the review queue.

Practice testing and distributed practice were rated high utility in the broad review by Dunlosky et al. (2013) [4].

05
A useful warning

Familiarity is
not mastery.

Rereading and highlighting can be useful for orientation—but they give you recognition, not proof that you can retrieve or apply knowledge under exam conditions.

  • Keep annotation brief and purposeful: mark structure, not every sentence.
  • Replace a second pass with a question: “What are the exceptions? What would change management?”
  • Measure learning by delayed, closed-book performance, not by how smooth the study session felt.
Evidence review
High utility

Practice testing and distributed practice stood out in a review of 10 common learning techniques. Rereading and highlighting received low-utility ratings.

Dunlosky et al., Psychological Science in the Public Interest (2013) [4]

06
Your next exam cycle

The one-page rule:
retrieve · space · mix

Every session

Retrieve first

Begin with 5–10 closed-book prompts from prior study before opening new material.

Every day

Review on time

Do a short queue of overdue questions; missed items return sooner than mastered ones.

Every week

Mix deliberately

Run a vignette set that crosses systems, then review errors by mechanism—not just answer key.

Start small. A sustainable daily retrieval habit beats an elaborate system that collapses in week two.
