How to Study

A short, research-backed guide to the techniques cognitive science actually supports — and the popular ones that quietly waste your time.

01
Retrieval practice

Test yourself,
don't re-read

Pulling an answer out of your memory strengthens it far more than putting information back in by re-reading.

  • Close the book and write what you remember, then check.
  • Use flashcards, practice questions, or a blank page.
  • The effort of retrieval is the learning — struggle is the point.
+50%
Students who were tested recalled about half as much again a week later as those who only re-read the same material.
— Roediger & Karpicke, 2006
02
The spacing effect

Space it out,
beat forgetting

Memory fades on a predictable curve. Reviewing at growing intervals resets the curve and locks knowledge in for the long term.

  • Study a little, often — not all at once.
  • Review after 1 day, then 3, then a week, then longer.
  • Beats cramming for durable, exam-day retention.
Each review (dots) slows the next drop-off.
Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, 1885
03
Mix it up

Interleave your
topics

Instead of drilling one topic to exhaustion (blocking), shuffle related topics together. It's harder now — and that's why it works.

  • Mixing forces you to choose the right strategy, not just repeat one.
  • Great for maths, problem sets, languages, and concepts that get confused.
  • Feels slower, but transfers far better to the real test.
Blocked — feels easy
AAA BBB CCC
Interleaved — learns better
ACB CAB BCA
04
Elaboration & the Feynman technique

Explain it simply,
ask why

Understanding grows when you connect new ideas to what you already know and put them in your own words.

  • Teach the idea aloud as if to a 12-year-old.
  • Where you stumble is exactly where your understanding is thin.
  • Keep asking “why?” and “how does this connect?”
The Feynman loop
  • Pick a concept & write it plainly
  • Explain it in simple language
  • Spot the gaps — go relearn them
  • Simplify again until it's obvious
05
The illusion of competence

Familiar isn't
the same as known

Highlighting and re-reading make text feel fluent and familiar, so your brain mistakes recognition for real understanding.

  • Rated among the least effective techniques in a major review.
  • Fluency ≠ mastery — you can't recall it when the page is gone.
  • Swap passive review for active recall.
low
Utility rating for highlighting/underlining and re-reading in Dunlosky et al.'s review of 10 study techniques — popular, but weak.
— Dunlosky et al., 2013
06
Do it this week

Your smarter-study
starter plan

Ditch the highlighter. After reading, close the book and write what you recall.
Build a question bank. Turn notes into flashcards or practice questions.
Schedule reviews. Revisit at 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, then fortnightly.
Interleave sessions. Mix 2–3 related topics instead of one long block.
Teach it out loud. Explain each idea simply; hunt down the gaps.
Space, don't cram. Short daily sessions beat one all-nighter.

Myth, debunked: “learning styles” (visual/auditory/kinesthetic) have no credible evidence. Matching teaching to a supposed style doesn't improve learning — so don't waste effort on it. Use the methods above instead.
